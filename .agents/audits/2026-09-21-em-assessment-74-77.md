# EM assessment — v2.56.74 → v2.56.77

**Date:** 2026-09-21 · **Subject:** the four releases that answered the
2026-09-21 audit. · **Conflict of interest:** I implemented them, so this is a
self-assessment and an independent consultant was commissioned in parallel.

## Bottom line

The engineering is correct, the security finding was real and material, and the
review discipline worked. But the effort was badly proportioned to the problem,
half the releases existed only to repair the previous one, and one of the four
artifacts I shipped will actively get in the team's way. Meanwhile the question
the whole exercise exists to answer — do notifications reach people now — is
still unanswered two days on, and answering it costs one minute.

## What it cost

| | |
|---|---|
| Product code changed | +68 / −22 lines, 2 files |
| Guards, scripts and CI added | +635 lines, 5 files |
| Releases | 4, in 124 minutes |
| Releases that only fixed the previous one | 2 of 4 |
| Releases containing no product change at all | 2 of 4 (2.56.76, 2.56.77) |

Nine lines of machinery for every line of fix. That ratio can be right when the
fix is one line in a system that must never regress. Here it went with a
50 percent rework rate, which says the machinery was not being built carefully
enough to justify its own size.

The sharpest version of the point, from the independent assessment: **every
defect the review loop caught was in a guard or a monitor, never in the product
fix.** The two product changes have held unchanged across all four releases. The
loop was catching complexity it had itself generated.

## What was done well

- **The security finding was real and correctly sized.** All thirteen exports of
  the notification module were reachable from a browser, and the read side
  returned any named user's bell, including an adopter's national ID number,
  phone and address. Closing it by removing the surface rather than adding a
  check was right: an authentication check would have broken the public contract
  and form routes, which call it without a session on purpose.
- **Review before production held, three times.** Every review found something
  real, and nothing defective reached production. That is the process working as
  intended, and it is a genuine change from the two audits before this one.
- **Mutation testing earned its place.** Every guard hole in this batch was found
  by putting the bug back and watching the guard pass, not by reading the code.

## What went wrong

**R1 · The action-count gate is mis-sized and will be neutralised within a week.**
`scripts/check-action-surface.mjs` fails the build when the number of
browser-callable endpoints changes from 148. Measured against this repository's
actual history: **43 commits across 20 distinct days in the last 90 days added
new server actions**, 37 of them adding just one or two as ordinary feature work.
So the gate breaks the build roughly every four or five days over entirely
correct changes. It will be bumped reflexively, and a threshold that is bumped
reflexively protects nothing. This is precisely the cry-wolf dynamic I argued
against when fixing the monitor, built into a CI gate in the same batch.
*Recommendation:* delete it, or downgrade it to a printed delta that does not
fail. The source-parse test already catches every realistic regression; the gate
adds only the renamed-wrapper case, which is not worth a build break every four
days.

**R2 · Half the releases were repairs.** 2.56.75 exists because 2.56.74's guard
had a one-line bypass. 2.56.77 exists because 2.56.76's monitor printed a
confident pass over data that proved nothing. Both were caught before
production, which is the system working — but two guards out of three shipped
with the same class of defect they were written to prevent. At some point that
stops being bad luck and becomes evidence that writing a bespoke guard per
finding is a low-yield activity at this scale.

**R3 · The monitor watches an event that happens 1.6 times a month.** Measured:
6 qualifying contributions in 112 days of production history. So the daily check
prints "proves nothing yet" on roughly nineteen days in twenty, can fire at most
about 1.6 times a month, and does so up to seven days late. Worse, **its alarming
half has never executed** — both real runs took the quiet path, so the exit
code, the label creation and the issue filing are untested end to end. A monitor
for a once-a-fortnight event, built before the underlying behaviour had been
observed working even once, is the wrong instrument. A one-minute probe was.

**R4 · The original problem is still unverified.** Two days after the fix,
production has recorded 8 audited actions, no notifications, no access requests,
no organization joins and no form submissions. None of the eight paths has run.
Four releases of guards and monitors have been built on top of a fix that nobody
has watched work. The one-minute test — add a contact detail to a record you own
and look for the log line — has been available the whole time.

**R5 · Three more artifacts need babysitting.** The array-parameter ratchet uses
exact list equality, so fixing one of its three recorded files breaks CI until
someone edits the list. The health check carries a hardcoded deploy timestamp.
The daily monitor has three documented paths where it goes red over correct
behaviour: an ownership transfer, an admin deleting a notification, and the
per-type kill switch. None is serious alone; together they are a standing tax.

## Residual risk, ranked by what would actually hurt

1. **Two live privacy leaks, still undecided.** A form-submission notification
   puts a full rescuer email address in a body sent to organization mates, and
   points them at a page the changelog itself calls owner-only
   (`src/app/api/form/[userId]/submit/route.ts:224-225`). Disclosed in the
   2.56.71 changelog, never decided either way. These are the only items on this
   list that can harm a real person today, and four releases went past them.
2. **The notification fix remains unproven in production.** If it is still
   broken, nobody has yet been told about a contribution to their record, and
   the monitor cannot tell you because no qualifying event has occurred.
3. **Five of the eight background sites cannot report failure.** They swallow
   their own errors and return normally, so a total failure reaches the wrapper
   looking like success. This is the observability gap the batch was meant to
   close, still open.
4. **The impersonation guard is still inert** beyond the re-export case now
   covered. The design is safe today; the test protecting it is thin.
5. Three files still bind an array to a database `IN` list, one of which feeds
   the dashboard counts. The audit log write inside the background task is
   still un-awaited, so those rows can be lost or carry a null address.

## One disagreement, recorded rather than resolved

The independent assessment says to **keep** the endpoint-count gate and accept a
"bump on purpose" tax, on the grounds that it reads compiled output and is the
only check that sees a new action wrapping a trusted helper. That reasoning is
sound in isolation. It did not measure the tax. I did: **43 commits across 20
distinct days in the last 90 days** added new server actions, 37 of them adding
one or two as routine feature work. So the tax is a broken build every four or
five days, paid by the same one person who would be bumping the number, and the
failure is a hard stop rather than a warning. My judgement is that a threshold
paid that often stops being read. Keeping it as a printed delta preserves the
signal and removes the stop; that is the version I would ship.

## Recommendation

1. **Run the one-minute verification today.** It answers the only question that
   matters and costs nothing. Everything else here is scaffolding around an
   unanswered question.
2. **Remove or downgrade the action-count gate before it fires.** It is the one
   thing in this batch that will make ordinary work worse.
3. **Decide the two privacy items** — fix them or accept them in writing. They
   are the only user-facing harm on the list and they have been open since
   2.56.71.
4. **Merge all four to production as one batch,** with a merge commit rather
   than a squash, and verify the deploy job rather than the merge: a cancelled
   end-to-end run silently skips the deploy. Note the daily monitor already runs
   from `staging`, which is the default branch, so it is not waiting on this.
5. **Fix the five sites that cannot report failure.** Letting the two fan-out
   helpers rethrow, and awaiting the audit write, is roughly ten lines and makes
   a good part of the monitor redundant.
6. **Then stop hardening this area.** The remaining findings are real but small,
   and this product sees about one sign-in a day. The next hour is better spent
   on the product than on the fourth guard protecting a path nobody has
   exercised.
